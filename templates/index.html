Motivation

Why Linearise?

In science and engineering, observed data rarely follow a straight line. Two of the most frequently encountered non-linear relationships are the exponential ($y = Ae^{bx}$) and the power law ($y = Ax^b$). Direct non-linear optimisation is computationally expensive and prone to local minima. Linearisation sidesteps these difficulties entirely.

The key insight is that both models become linear after a logarithmic transformation. Once linearised, we apply the classical ordinary least-squares (OLS) normal equations — which have a unique, closed-form solution — then transform the result back to original coordinates.

◈
Core Principle A non-linear model is linearisable if it can be written as $\ln y = c + bX$ for some transformation $X$ of the independent variable. The slope $b$ and intercept $c$ are then recoverable by OLS.

1 — Exponential Regression: $y = Ae^{bx}$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields:

$$\ln y = \ln A + bx$$

Substituting $Y_i = \ln y_i$ and $c = \ln A$, this is exactly the linear model $Y = bx + c$. We solve for $b$ and $c$ using the standard OLS normal equations on the $n$ transformed pairs $(x_i, Y_i)$:

$$b \;=\; \frac{n\displaystyle\sum x_i Y_i \;-\; \sum x_i \sum Y_i}{n\displaystyle\sum x_i^2 \;-\; \bigl(\sum x_i\bigr)^2} \qquad c \;=\; \bar{Y} - b\bar{x} \qquad A = e^{\,c}$$
SymbolMeaningRole in Model
$A$Prefactor / amplitudeValue of $y$ at $x = 0$; scale factor
$b$Rate constant$b > 0$ growth, $b < 0$ decay; units: per unit of $x$
$Y_i$$\ln y_i$Linearised response variable
$c$$\ln A$OLS intercept in log-space
ℹ
When to use exponential regression Apply when data exhibit constant percentage growth or decay — e.g. bacterial population, radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, compound interest.

2 — Power-Law Regression: $y = Ax^b$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$\ln y = \ln A + b\ln x$$

Substituting $Y_i = \ln y_i$, $X_i = \ln x_i$, and $c = \ln A$, we obtain $Y = bX + c$. Identical normal equations apply:

$$b \;=\; \frac{n\displaystyle\sum X_i Y_i \;-\; \sum X_i \sum Y_i}{n\displaystyle\sum X_i^2 \;-\; \bigl(\sum X_i\bigr)^2} \qquad c \;=\; \bar{Y} - b\bar{X} \qquad A = e^{\,c}$$
SymbolMeaningRole in Model
$A$Scale coefficientControls overall magnitude
$b$Scaling exponent$b = 2$ → quadratic; $b = 0.5$ → square-root
$X_i$$\ln x_i$Linearised predictor variable
$Y_i$$\ln y_i$Linearised response variable
ℹ
When to use power-law regression Applies when data on a log–log plot appear linear. Common in fluid drag ($F \propto v^2$), allometry (organ mass vs. body mass), Zipf's law, and fractal scaling.

3 — Goodness of Fit: $R^2$

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ measures the proportion of variance in $y$ explained by the fitted model. It is computed in the original (untransformed) space for an honest appraisal:

$$R^2 \;=\; 1 - \frac{SS_\text{res}}{SS_\text{tot}} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$
RangeInterpretation
$R^2 = 1.000$Perfect fit — model passes exactly through all data points
$R^2 \ge 0.99$Excellent — typical for clean physical measurements
$0.90 \le R^2 < 0.99$Good — may still have systematic residual pattern
$R^2 < 0.90$Poor — consider a different model or transformation
$R^2 < 0$Model is worse than predicting the mean; serious misfit
⚑
Important caveat $R^2$ computed in log-space (from the linearised model) overestimates fit quality because logs compress large values. Always report $R^2$ calculated from the original-scale residuals $y_i - \hat{y}_i$.

4 — Assumptions and Limitations

Requirements
  • All $y_i > 0$ (both models; log undefined otherwise)
  • All $x_i > 0$ additionally for power law
  • At least $n \ge 3$ data points (preferably $n \ge 6$)
  • Sufficient spread in $x$ to define curvature
Limitation of Linearisation
  • OLS in log-space minimises relative errors, not absolute
  • Small $y$ values dominate the fit after log transformation
  • Outliers in log-space can be extreme in original scale
  • Confidence intervals require back-transformation with care

5 — Complete Step-by-Step Algorithm

Validate inputs. Confirm all $y_i > 0$. For power-law, also confirm all $x_i > 0$. Require $n \ge 3$. Check for duplicate $x$ values and obvious data entry errors.

Apply log transformation. Compute $Y_i = \ln y_i$ for all $i$. For power-law, also compute $X_i = \ln x_i$. For exponential, $X_i = x_i$ (no transform on $x$).

Compute the four required sums: $\sum X_i$, $\sum Y_i$, $\sum X_i^2$, and $\sum X_i Y_i$. These are the only quantities needed for the normal equations.

Solve the normal equations. Calculate slope $b$ using the quotient formula. Compute means $\bar{X}$ and $\bar{Y}$, then intercept $c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{X}$.

Back-transform the intercept. Recover $A = e^c$. The model is now fully defined: $y = Ae^{bx}$ or $y = Ax^b$ depending on choice.

Evaluate fit quality. Compute $\hat{y}_i = Ae^{bx_i}$ (or $Ax_i^b$) for each observation. Calculate residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Compute $SS_\text{res}$, $SS_\text{tot}$, and $R^2$.

Inspect residuals. Plot $e_i$ vs. $x_i$ or $\hat{y}_i$. Residuals should appear random and centred near zero. Any systematic pattern (curvature, funnel shape) indicates model misfit or a different functional form is needed.

Radioactive Decay

A Geiger counter records activity $A(t)$ (counts/second) of a short-lived isotope. We fit $y = Ae^{bx}$ with $b < 0$ to determine the half-life.

Compute sums ($n = 6$):

$$\sum x_i = 75 \qquad \sum Y_i = 37.3607 \qquad \sum x_i^2 = 1375 \qquad \sum x_i Y_i = 431.8565$$

Normal equations:

$$b = \frac{6(431.8565) - (75)(37.3607)}{6(1375) - (75)^2} = \frac{2591.139 - 2802.053}{8250 - 5625} = \frac{-210.914}{2625} \approx \mathbf{-0.08035}$$
$$c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{x} = 6.2268 - (-0.08035)(12.5) = 6.2268 + 1.0044 = \mathbf{7.2312}$$ $$A = e^{7.2312} \approx \mathbf{1381.3}$$

Half-life from rate constant:

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|b|} = \frac{0.6931}{0.08035} \approx \mathbf{8.63 \text{ minutes}}$$
Fitted Model
$y = 1381.3\;e^{-0.08035\,x}$,   $t_{1/2} \approx 8.63$ min
◈
Interpretation The fitted initial activity $A \approx 1381$ cps slightly exceeds the measured 1200 cps at $t = 0$, which is typical: OLS minimises the sum of squared residuals over all points, not just the first. The half-life of ≈ 8.6 minutes is consistent with several medical isotopes used in PET imaging.

Quick Reference

Formula Sheet

Exponential: $y = Ae^{bx}$

$$Y_i = \ln y_i \qquad X_i = x_i$$ $$b = \frac{n\sum x_i Y_i - \sum x_i \sum Y_i}{n\sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2} \qquad c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{x} \qquad A = e^c$$

Power Law: $y = Ax^b$

$$Y_i = \ln y_i \qquad X_i = \ln x_i$$ $$b = \frac{n\sum X_i Y_i - \sum X_i \sum Y_i}{n\sum X_i^2 - (\sum X_i)^2} \qquad c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{X} \qquad A = e^c$$

Goodness of Fit

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2} \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{|b|} \text{ (exponential decay)}$$

Terminology

Glossary

Linearisation
Transforming a non-linear model into a linear one so that OLS can be applied directly.
Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)
Minimises the sum of squared vertical residuals. Yields a unique analytical solution via normal equations.
Prefactor (A)
The back-transformed intercept $A = e^c$. For exponential, it equals $y$ at $x = 0$.
Rate Constant (b)
In exponential models, $b$ controls growth ($b>0$) or decay ($b<0$) per unit $x$.
Scaling Exponent (b)
In power-law models, $b$ is the log-log slope. $b=1$ is linear, $b=2$ quadratic, $b=0.5$ square-root.
R² (Coefficient of Determination)
Fraction of variance in $y$ explained by the model. Computed in original (not log) space.
SS_res (Residual Sum of Squares)
$\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$ — total squared distance between observations and fitted values.
SS_tot (Total Sum of Squares)
$\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2$ — total variance of the response variable around its mean.
Half-Life
For exponential decay: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / |b|$. Time for $y$ to halve from any initial value.
Doubling Time
For exponential growth: $T_d = \ln 2 / b$. Time for $y$ to double from any initial value.
Residual
$e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Positive = under-prediction, negative = over-prediction by model.
Back-Transformation
Converting OLS results from log-space back to original units: $A = e^c$, with $b$ unchanged.

Decision Guide

Which Model to Choose?

Exponential $y = Ae^{bx}$

Use when:

  • Growth/decay at a constant percentage rate
  • Semi-log plot ($\ln y$ vs $x$) is approximately linear
  • $x$ can be zero or negative (population time, temperature)
  • Variable $x$ is time, voltage, or temperature

Examples: bacteria, radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, Newton cooling, compound interest

Power Law $y = Ax^b$

Use when:

  • Log–log plot ($\ln y$ vs $\ln x$) is approximately linear
  • All $x > 0$ (ratio or scale variable)
  • Relationship follows a scaling law
  • $b$ has a physical interpretation (area=2, volume=3)

Examples: drag force, allometry, Kepler's third law, Zipf, fractal dimension

⚑
Diagnostic plots Always inspect both a semi-log plot and a log–log plot of your data before choosing a model. Whichever appears more linear indicates the correct model family. Do not rely on $R^2$ alone — a high $R^2$ is necessary but not sufficient.
