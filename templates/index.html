Motivation

Why Linearise?

In science and engineering, observed data rarely follow a straight line. Two of the most frequently encountered non-linear relationships are the exponential ($y = Ae^{bx}$) and the power law ($y = Ax^b$). Linearisation sidesteps expensive non-linear optimisation entirely — once transformed, the unique closed-form OLS solution applies directly.

◈
Core Principle A non-linear model is linearisable if it can be written as $\ln y = c + bX$ for some transformation $X$. The slope $b$ and intercept $c$ are then recoverable by OLS, with $A = e^c$ by back-transformation.

1 — Exponential: $y = Ae^{bx}$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$\ln y = \ln A + bx$$

Setting $Y_i = \ln y_i$ and $c = \ln A$, this becomes $Y = bx + c$ — a straight line. The OLS normal equations on $(x_i, Y_i)$:

$$b = \frac{n\sum x_i Y_i - \sum x_i \sum Y_i}{n\sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2} \qquad c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{x} \qquad A = e^c$$

$A$Prefactor / amplitudeValue of $y$ at $x = 0$

ℹ
When to use Constant percentage growth/decay — bacteria, radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, compound interest.

2 — Power Law: $y = Ax^b$

$$\ln y = \ln A + b\ln x$$

Setting $Y_i = \ln y_i$, $X_i = \ln x_i$, $c = \ln A$ → $Y = bX + c$. Same normal equations:

$$b = \frac{n\sum X_i Y_i - \sum X_i \sum Y_i}{n\sum X_i^2 - (\sum X_i)^2} \qquad c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{X} \qquad A = e^c$$
ℹ
When to use Log–log plot appears linear. Fluid drag, allometry, Kepler's third law, Zipf's law, fractal scaling.

3 — Goodness of Fit: $R^2$

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_\text{res}}{SS_\text{tot}} = 1 - \frac{\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$
RangeInterpretation
$R^2 = 1.000$Perfect — model passes through all data points
$\ge 0.99$Excellent — typical for clean physical data
$0.90$–$0.99$Good — may have systematic residual pattern
$< 0.90$Poor — consider a different model
$< 0$Model is worse than predicting the mean
⚑
Important caveat Always compute $R^2$ in the original space, not log-space. Log-transform compresses large values and inflates apparent fit quality.

4 — Assumptions & Limitations

Requirements
  • All $y_i > 0$ (both models)
  • All $x_i > 0$ additionally for power law
  • At least $n \ge 3$ data points
  • Sufficient spread in $x$ to define curvature
Limitations of Linearisation
  • OLS in log-space minimises relative errors
  • Small $y$ values dominate after log transform
  • Outliers in log-space can be extreme in original scale
  • Confidence intervals require careful back-transformation

5 — Step-by-Step Algorithm

Validate. Confirm all $y_i > 0$. For power-law, also confirm all $x_i > 0$. Require $n \ge 3$.

Apply log transformation. Compute $Y_i = \ln y_i$. For power-law, also $X_i = \ln x_i$.

Compute four sums. $\sum X_i$, $\sum Y_i$, $\sum X_i^2$, $\sum X_iY_i$ — the only quantities needed.

Solve normal equations. Calculate slope $b$, then intercept $c = \bar{Y} - b\bar{X}$.

Back-transform. Recover $A = e^c$. The model is now fully defined.

Evaluate. Compute $\hat{y}_i$, residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$, then $SS_\text{res}$, $SS_\text{tot}$, $R^2$.

Inspect residuals. Plot $e_i$ vs $x_i$. Residuals should appear random around zero. Systematic pattern → model misfit.

Quick Reference

Formula Sheet

Exponential: $y = Ae^{bx}$

$$Y_i=\ln y_i,\quad X_i=x_i$$ $$b=\frac{n\sum xY-\sum x\sum Y}{n\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2}$$ $$c=\bar{Y}-b\bar{x}\qquad A=e^c$$

Power Law: $y = Ax^b$

$$Y_i=\ln y_i,\quad X_i=\ln x_i$$ $$b=\frac{n\sum XY-\sum X\sum Y}{n\sum X^2-(\sum X)^2}$$ $$c=\bar{Y}-b\bar{X}\qquad A=e^c$$

Goodness of Fit

$$R^2=1-\frac{\sum(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2}$$

Derived Quantities

$$t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{|b|}\quad(\text{decay})$$ $$T_d=\frac{\ln2}{b}\quad(\text{growth})$$

Terminology

Glossary

Linearisation
Transforming a non-linear model into a linear one so OLS can be applied directly.
OLS (Ordinary Least Squares)
Minimises sum of squared vertical residuals; yields a unique analytical solution.
Prefactor (A)
Back-transformed intercept $A = e^c$. For exponential, equals $y$ at $x = 0$.
Rate Constant (b)
Controls growth ($b > 0$) or decay ($b < 0$) per unit $x$ in exponential models.
Scaling Exponent (b)
Log-log slope in power-law. $b=1$ linear, $b=2$ quadratic, $b=0.5$ square-root.
R² (Coefficient of Determination)
Fraction of variance explained. Always compute in original (not log) space.
SS_res
Residual sum of squares: $\sum(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$.
SS_tot
Total sum of squares: $\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2$.
Half-Life
For exponential decay: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / |b|$. Time for $y$ to halve.
Doubling Time
For exponential growth: $T_d = \ln 2 / b$. Time for $y$ to double.
Residual
$e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. Positive = under-prediction, negative = over-prediction.
Back-Transformation
Converting OLS result from log-space: $A = e^c$, $b$ unchanged.

Decision Guide

Which Model?

Exponential $y = Ae^{bx}$
  • Constant percentage rate of change
  • Semi-log plot ($\ln y$ vs $x$) looks linear
  • $x$ can be zero or negative
  • Time, voltage, or temperature as predictor
Power Law $y = Ax^b$
  • Log–log plot ($\ln y$ vs $\ln x$) looks linear
  • All $x > 0$ required
  • Relationship follows a scaling law
  • $b$ has a physical interpretation
⚑
Diagnostic plots first Always inspect both semi-log and log–log plots before choosing. High $R^2$ is necessary but not sufficient.
